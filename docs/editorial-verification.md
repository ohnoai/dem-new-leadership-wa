## Findings and permitted wording

### New Democratic majorities

**Supported:** "Democrats won new majorities in the House and Senate." NPR reports control of both chambers based on its analysis of Associated Press race calls; NBC independently projects control of both chambers. These are reported election calls, not an audit of certified returns.[1][2]

**Temporal distinction:** The electoral victory does not mean the new majorities already govern on November 10. NBC says Democrats will take control in January. Describe the new Congress or the incoming majorities, not Democrats' present control of the sitting Congress.[2]

No exact seat count is needed or verified here. Do not turn a projected tally into a certified final result or imply that all races have been called.

### Leadership choices are still ahead

**House:** NBC's November 4 live-blog item reports leadership elections beginning November 18 at 9 a.m. and continuing through November 19. Bloomberg Government's readable November 4 opening independently reports November 18 and 19; its remaining article was not accessible in this retrieval. ABC's November 9 report says November 18. These support an upcoming House caucus selection, with November 18 and 19 attributed to NBC/Bloomberg, not a claim to have read an official scheduling notice.[2][9][7]

**Senate:** NBC reports that Senate Democrats expect December elections without an exact date announced. ABC still reports no announced date on November 9. The published Democratic Conference rules provide for officer elections beginning no earlier than December before the next Congress, or when a vacancy occurs. An exact 2026 election date remains unverified.[2][7][4]

**Candidates are not winners:** Jeffries' November 4 official letter requests support for his candidacy for Speaker. NBC describes Schumer predicting his own reelection. Neither a candidacy nor a prediction establishes that the upcoming election has already occurred or guarantees its outcome.[3][2]

Safe date-light wording is "before the upcoming Democratic leadership elections." If specific dates are published in campaign copy, attribute the House schedule and recheck it before release. Do not invent a Senate date.

### Caucus nomination is not election as Speaker

The House Democratic Caucus rules say it nominates a candidate for Speaker. The full House's separate Speaker election is normally a public roll call, with Members naming their preferred candidate aloud. A November caucus nomination therefore does not itself confer the Speakership.[8][5]

The House caucus rules provide secret ballots with explicit exceptions, including a motion to waive secrecy. Senate Democratic rules require secret ballots for contested conference leadership elections. Do not describe every possible leadership selection as necessarily secret.[8][4]

Senate party leaders are chosen by their respective conferences. The Democratic Conference rules say conference officers are selected solely by the Conference, with no Senate action required. There is no corresponding full-Senate Speaker-style confirmation of the Democratic Leader.[6][4]

Do not use an unconditional numerical Speaker threshold. CRS explains that the required majority is of votes cast for a person by name, not necessarily a majority of the entire authorized membership.[5]

### Individual votes and campaign demands

This research establishes no Washington recipient's personal vote in any secret leadership ballot. Do not write "you voted for" a leader based only on a collective caucus result, party membership, a reported endorsement, or a candidate's confidence. Public statements of support and actual recorded floor votes are different evidence and would need their own dated sources.

Prospective requests such as "support new leadership," "oppose this nomination," or "state your position publicly" are campaign demands, not assertions about a past ballot. Elected state officials should be asked to advocate publicly, not described as having a congressional caucus or Speaker vote.

Arguments that existing leadership has failed, that voters deserve different leaders, or that a different strategy is needed are campaign judgments. The sources below establish electoral and procedural facts, not those judgments.
